# TOOL-111: A Daily Learning Workflow

## Descriptions

- Offered by: A community practice summary contributed by @HardwayLinka
- Prerequisites: Browsers and document editors; any existing note-taking habit will do, no prior tool experience required
- Programming Languages: None; involves Markdown and light tool configuration
- Difficulty: 🌟🌟
- Class Hour: About 3 hours to grasp the overall idea, roughly 15 hours to assemble the workflow, then continuous refinement through use

Computer knowledge is broad and fast moving, so lifelong learning is close to unavoidable. Yet the sources are as scattered as they are fragmented: hundreds of pages of manuals, two-sentence blog posts, and the news item that happens to scroll past your phone. Fragments are not the problem; the problem is that they stay scattered and cannot be found again when you want to review. This course addresses exactly that: collecting knowledge from different sources into your own storehouse with a suitable set of tools, so it is easy to consult and revisit.

The underlying logic comes down to two points. First, **prefer sources with less information loss**: roughly, source code over official documentation, official documentation over English books, English books over English blogs, and English blogs over Chinese blogs. This is not a dismissal of second-hand material, which often carries the author's own transformations, such as flowcharts, mind maps, analogies, and connections to other topics, and helps you grasp the core faster, much like the study guides used in school. It simply means you should move upstream when accuracy and freshness matter. Second, **learn recursively**. Real situations rarely center on a single topic, and you will run into new terms, classic papers you have not read, and code you do not understand; dig into them and connect the knowledge points into a network instead of walking around them.

The workflow skeleton is built on these two points. For a note app I settled on Obsidian for three reasons: it is local-first, opens instantly, and can also store e-books; it is Markdown-based, so notes are not locked into one product and can be opened by other tools; and its plugin ecosystem is large and active, with popular plugins iterating continuously, unifying all kinds of sources in one place. Before that I tried Roam Research and Logseq, both outline-based, and outlines either stretch notes vertically or consume the width once nesting grows, which put me off; Notion opened too slowly and was dropped too.

Collecting information raises two problems: formats and conversion. Daily sources fall into articles, papers, e-books, and courses, with formats mainly web pages, PDF, mobi, azw, and azw3. Web articles can be clipped to Markdown, and I use JianYue, which clips articles from nearly any platform and imports them into Obsidian; papers and e-books that are already PDF are easiest, and other formats can be converted with calibre. Then comes managing what you have collected: file-type resources live locally or in the cloud, web resources can go into browser bookmarks or be captured across devices with Cubox, where a swipe on your phone saves a page and the free plan's hundred-item limit actually nudges you to digest them. For personal sites with no mobile app that bookmarks forget, use RSS: discover feeds with RSSHub Radar on desktop and subscribe with Feedly.

Processing is the key to internalizing. For English material, use Quicker with Salazhi Dictionary for selection-based translation, covering words, sentences, and paragraphs in one tool instead of juggling several. When you are not in a hurry, the Collins dictionary explains English in English and sits closer to how the language is actually thought. For English videos, Language Reactor exports subtitles with Chinese translations from YouTube and Netflix, which you can paste into Obsidian and read as an article; the Media Extended plugin inserts links that jump to a specific timestamp, tying each subtitle line to its picture so you no longer need to cut clips by hand. Combined with Obsidian's built-in links and the Annotator plugin, notes, PDFs, and videos all become jump targets, and the processing chain is complete.

Review goes to spaced repetition. Obsidian has a plugin connecting to Anki that exports note fragments as cards, each linking back to the original passage. Learning is like climbing a mountain, unfamiliar and tiring; review is like descending, with none of the unfamiliarity but no way to skip it.

One final reminder matters more than the tools themselves: do not force tools into your workflow for a vague sense of satisfaction. This workflow grew out of two years of spare-time learning, pushed bit by bit by the tedium of repetitive work. Pain points come first, tools second. Life is short, and doing real work matters most.

## Course Resources

- Course Website: The official Obsidian documentation and plugin marketplace, alongside the docs for RSSHub, Feedly, JianYue, and Cubox
- Textbooks: For further reading, the companion pieces *A 3000+ Hour Learning Workflow* and *Advanced Obsidian: Notes That Jump to Any Format*
- Recordings: Mostly written material; no videos needed
- Assignments: No standardized assignments; build only the clip-to-note step first, and add stages once it runs smoothly

## Personal Resources

A sensible order: install Obsidian and set a directory convention, configure web clipping, then add video subtitles and PDF jumps, and finally wire in Anki. Run each stage for a week or two and confirm it is genuinely useful before continuing, rather than installing a pile of plugins none of which you know well.
