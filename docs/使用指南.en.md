# How to Use This Guide

As more and more contributors join, the content of this book keeps expanding. Finishing every course in it is neither realistic nor necessary; trying to do so may even backfire and leave you with much effort and little reward. To better match the readers and make this book genuinely useful, I have roughly divided readers into the following three groups according to their needs, so that each of you can plan a self-study program that fits your own situation precisely.

## Just Started University

If you have just entered university, or are still in your early years and are majoring in computer science or hoping to switch into it, consider yourself lucky, because studying is your main job. You have plenty of time and freedom to learn what interests you, without the pressure of work or the trivialities of daily life, and you need not agonize over utilitarian questions like "will this be useful?" or "will this get me a job?". So how should you arrange your university studies? The first thing, I think, is to break with the **step-by-step, follow-the-textbook** habit of high school. University is free, in both life and study; there are still classes and exams, but success no longer comes from merely grinding through problem sets. First, you are not limited to the textbook or the classroom — even within your own field you can explore far beyond the syllabus, and you can also learn across disciplines. Second, I hope you will be a little less utilitarian. Having a goal (graduate school, employment, studying abroad) is good, but do not act only to reach it; try instead to learn, in the process, things that will stay with you for a lifetime — a learning method, perhaps, or a way of doing things. Still, being less utilitarian should not become an excuse for laziness and self-indulgence. Finally, balance study and life: studying should be a part of life. In my undergraduate days I joined a club with some almost fanatical people who even slept on the floor of the club room every night; I do not think that was genuine devotion to knowledge, but rather a lack of maturity in how they lived.

This book covers knowledge across the whole field of computing. I suggest you follow your degree plan and take the courses corresponding to your year, while using your spare time to explore courses the classroom does not offer — especially tools and techniques such as Git, Docker and Vue. As things stand, CS education in China does not teach much practical knowledge, yet practice is the most important part of learning in this field, so you will most likely have to do it yourself and accumulate experience bit by bit.

The first thing is to understand what "elegant code" means. Many introductory programming courses in China easily turn into a dry list of syntax rules, less effective than simply having students read the official documentation. In fact, it is very helpful to get a feel, right at the start of programming, for what counts as elegant code and what code "has bad taste". Introductory courses usually begin with procedural programming, such as C, but even in the procedural style you should not lose the habits of modularity and encapsulation. If you write code only to pass an online judge, copying and pasting to pile up one giant `main` function for convenience, your code quality will quickly settle at that level. Once you meet a slightly bigger project, the cost of debugging, communication and maintenance will snowball. So as you write, ask yourself a few questions: is there a lot of duplication? Is this function doing too many things? The Linux style is to make each function do one thing well. Can this logic be extracted into a separate function? At first it will feel awkward, and you may doubt whether a simple problem is worth all this trouble, but the value of good habits is long-term. C itself is not hard — even a middle schooler can learn it — so why would a company hire you to be a programmer?

After procedural programming, the second semester usually moves on to object orientation, such as C++ or Java. I highly recommend the Notes of MIT 6.031: Software Construction, which uses Java (TypeScript since 2022) to explain in detail how to write more elegant code: test-driven development, the design of function specifications, exception handling, and so on. Since you are touching object orientation, it is also worth learning some common design patterns. Object-oriented courses in China sometimes degenerate into syntax lessons, making students agonize over every way of writing inheritance, with puzzles that resemble brain teasers — things you will almost never use in a real project. What object orientation really trains is the ability to abstract a real problem into a set of classes and the relationships among them; design patterns are abstract lessons distilled by those who came before. *Big Talk Design Patterns* (《大话设计模式》) is written in a simple, accessible way and makes a good introduction.

The second thing is to learn tools and skills that clearly boost productivity, such as Git, Shell and Vim. I strongly recommend MIT's Missing Semester. It will feel very unfamiliar at first, but force yourself to use these tools for a while, and once you are comfortable your development efficiency will step up noticeably. Many small tools help too. One principle of mine is: any action that takes your hands off the keyboard is worth eliminating. Switching apps, opening files, browsing the web — all can become shortcuts through plugins or tools; on macOS, Alfred is one example. If you repeat an operation every day, and each time it costs more than a second, find a way to cut it to 0.1 seconds. You will be dealing with computers for the next few decades, so forming a smooth workflow early will save you a great deal of effort. Also, learn to touch-type. If you still have to look at the keyboard, find a tutorial right away — it makes a big difference for your efficiency.

The third thing is to balance coursework and self-study. You may question the status quo, but you still have to respect the rules — the GPA carries considerable weight for graduate school admission, after all. So in your first year I would still suggest following your timetable as much as possible, supplemented by high-quality outside resources. For calculus and linear algebra, for example, you can consult the Notes of MIT 18.01/18.02 and MIT 18.06; in the holidays you can learn Python with UCB CS61A. At the same time, gradually build up the programming habits and practical skills mentioned above. In my own experience, mathematics courses carry a high share of credits in the first year, and the exam style varies enormously by school and teacher. Self-study may help you understand the mathematics itself, but it does not necessarily earn you a good grade. So before exams, work through past papers with clear focus to cover the exam-taking side. Once you reach your second year, you can steadily raise the share of self-study, which is likely the safer path.

From the second year on, computer science courses pile up, and that is actually a good time to shift your center of gravity toward self-study. I have organized my three years of self-teaching into a CS study list, briefly describing what each course covers and why it is worth taking. Most of the courses on your timetable will have a foreign counterpart in this list, usually of more solid quality. The core knowledge of computer science is shared to begin with, and good courses tend to explain the principles thoroughly, which many read-the-slides classrooms simply cannot do. Generally speaking, if you pull out the slides the teacher used all semester and cram for two days before the exam, your written score will not look too bad. If a course has a big assignment, you can adapt the labs or projects of a foreign course to meet the course requirements. In short, be flexible: the goal is to master knowledge more efficiently, and when a rule conflicts with that goal, find a way around it or simplify.

Finally, I hope you will be a little less restless and utilitarian, and a little more patient with genuine interest. Many people ask me whether self-study requires exceptional willpower. I think it comes down to what you actually want. If your plan is just that learning a programming language will get you a salary of ten thousand a month, and you are chasing the internet boom, no amount of advice from me will help. When I first started self-studying, I did not think much about utility; it was mostly curiosity, a wish to understand certain things. The whole process was not as grim as it sounds — eat when it is time to eat, play when it is time to play — and looking back, the materials had accumulated all by themselves. Today Sino-American competition is the backdrop, and we are still learning from high-quality foreign courses, admiring how well others do it and feeling a touch of crisis at the same time. So who will change things from here? It still comes down to you, the newcomers. So go for it, young one.

## Keep the Best, Drop the Rest

If you have already finished your undergraduate degree, are studying for a master's, or are already working — perhaps in another field entirely and hoping to make a career change into programming in your spare time — then you probably cannot squeeze out enough time to work systematically through *A Learning Plan for Reference Only* from beginning to end. Yet you genuinely want to fill in the foundations you missed as an undergraduate. Since readers in this group usually already have some programming experience, introductory courses can be skipped. From a practical standpoint, your career direction is largely settled, and there is no need to go deep into every branch of computing; what is more worth investing in are the principles and skills that generalize widely. So, drawing on my own experience, I have picked a few core courses that I consider the most important and the highest in quality, in the hope of helping you understand computers more deeply. Once you have finished these, whatever your job, you should no longer be someone who merely calls libraries, but someone with a clearer picture of how computers really work underneath.

## **Dreams Have Their Own Destination**

If you have a solid command of the core courses in computing and have already settled on your work or research direction, the book still holds many courses that are not mentioned in [A Learning Plan for Reference Only](AI-CS学习规划.md) for you to explore.

As more contributors join, new branches will keep appearing in the table of contents on the left, such as **Advanced Machine Learning** and **Machine Learning Systems**. Under the same branch there are several courses of the same kind, each with a different emphasis and different labs — the **Operating Systems** branch, for example, includes courses from MIT, Berkeley, Nanjing University and Harbin Institute of Technology. If you want to go deep into a field, studying these parallel courses will give you different perspectives on similar knowledge. The author also plans to invite researchers in relevant fields to share the study and research paths of particular subfields, so that this guide can grow in depth while it expands in breadth.
